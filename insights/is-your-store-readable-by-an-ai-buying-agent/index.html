Is Your Store Readable by an AI Buying Agent?

Yev Specter · November 16, 2026 · 12 min read

Let's say you own an online store that sells car parts. Someone is looking for a driver's side mirror for a 2021 Sprinter, a heated one, and you have that exact mirror in stock. This person needs it by Friday. It's urgent, because their workshop called and told them that if they want to pick up the van by Friday, the part has to be delivered. They need the van. It's their work car. They don't have the time to browse the internet themselves, so they decide to outsource it to an AI assistant. They give the assistant the specs, based on what the workshop told them, and ask it to find the part and purchase it for them.

From your standpoint, your listing has all the information. It says the mirror is heated. But there are other options for heated mirrors on your site. The marketplace says the part is in stock, and your website says it's in stock and ships on request. Both can be true. But the assistant gets conflicting information. It cannot tell which mirror out of a few similar ones is actually heated, and which one can actually be delivered by Friday.

Now imagine a different situation. The same car owner has the time, and they visit your website, and they also find your company on some marketplace, and they see this conflict in information. The first instinct for them, when they are reviewing all this personally, is to call you. So they call you, or text you, or email you. But the issue with AI assistants is that they don't call. What they do is focus on clarity, and they go to a seller that has the answer. So in this situation the customer never sees your listing, and they could have seen it if they had done it personally. You never get the call, so you can never learn what was actually missing for the assistant to find you and to pick your store.

What does your store actually tell the assistant?

There are three ways AI assistants can get your information. The first one is the product page. That's what you were probably focusing on when building your online store. The product page is built for people, and very often product details are stored in photos or tabs and might not be immediately readable or visible. There's also the feed. That's a list of everything you sell. Think of it as a spreadsheet, one row per item, sent out on a schedule. What's important to remember is that a feed is a copy, and copies get outdated. So the marketplace can say in stock, and your site says ships on request. The third way is an integration. That's like a direct line between your store and an AI assistant.

You probably already have a number of listings that get the most customer questions. Going back to our example, it could be something regarding a connector on that heated mirror. Think of where the answers live today. It could be a picture, it could be a supplier email, or it could be your team member that you usually go to if someone needs clarity on a certain group of products. If something is hidden there, that's the first thing that has to be fixed. You need to provide answers for all the variants you sell. There has to be one stable ID for each variant, so that the feed and the cart are aligned. It's very useful to ask yourself, what does someone need to know to decide to buy this particular item?

It's also important to clarify the stock number you're selling against. If you're selling in multiple countries, check your currency settings so that the pricing looks right. The delivery estimate for the customer's address has to be clear and mentioned for this particular item. There's another important clarification to provide regarding warranty and returns. Very often it's mentioned somewhere where it's not immediately readable, and it could be unclear for this item.

Make sure that your site and feed do not say different things. If you have a support team, they probably trust a single source of truth. Try to understand why exactly they rely on this particular source. If you are a small shop, your first go-to destination is the catalog. Fix it. Build and improve the links to your inventory. If you are selling in several countries, identify if there are any discrepancies in the data, who takes care of the data in each market, and who gets alerted in each country. You don't need all three on day one. To be found and recommended, start with cleaning up your feed and structuring your pages against how clear they are. If at some point you decide that your online business is ready to let AI assistants buy, then the live connection, the integration, is the answer.

A product listing can't approve a purchase

In 2025, OpenAI introduced Instant Checkout, which allows shoppers to buy inside ChatGPT. In March 2026, they changed their approach to focusing more on helping people find products and making it more flexible for stores by keeping their own checkout. For you as a store owner, it means more control, but it also means that you need to have an internal framework for the buying process. OpenAI has also issued a guide for merchants, explaining that the feed is used for finding, but the final check happens at purchase. It could be through your site or integration.

There is time that passes between finding and buying, and the price can change and can be different now. Regarding our example, that last heated mirror may be gone. It's important that your store has internal checks before accepting the order. These checks go over exact variant, price, or if the item is in stock. The assistant cannot decide if it should get a standard mirror instead of heated. If it understands that there is something outside of what the customer approved, it goes back to the customer and double checks with them.

Google's Agent Payments Protocol has a similar idea. Two permissions, for finding and buying. It also allows you to set permission in advance, but with certain limitations. If you are just starting to prepare your store for AI buying assistants, my advice is the customer has to confirm every purchase.

Let's assume your customer gave their assistant permission to buy something. First of all, it's your store's choice and your decision as the store owner if you are willing to accept or not to accept orders like this. But if you decide that your store is ready to work with orders like this one, the permissions need to be specific. By that, I mean exact products, how much the assistant can spend, how long the permission is valid for, and how the customer can cancel it. It's very useful to keep a record of what your customers working with AI assistants approve. If there's a disputed charge and the customer says, "I never ordered this", you need to have a clear record of such a purchase. All normal payment checks and your internal fraud checks stay.

Where I'd start

I recommend running a 20-product test. You as a store owner can probably easily identify 20 products that matter the most to your business. Give them to a person who doesn't work on the catalog or, even better, doesn't know anything about your business, but could be one of your customers. Give this person the information an assistant would get. Their task would be to identify the right variant, whether the total price is clear, whether the arrival date is there, and if they can easily understand what's going to happen if the product doesn't fit. Every time they have questions, or they need to ask your team, write down what's missing. It's important at this point that you fix it at the source, so the site and the feed both get updated. This is a quick and doable test that is going to take you one afternoon, but this is where you will gain clarity of where you stand in terms of your store's readiness to be picked by AI shopping assistants.

Move in stages and start with being found or preparing a cart. Leave payment for later. Before you move to the payment phase, try to get as much proof as possible that the customer agreed for the assistant to perform certain actions. Make sure you have an order record and that you have a way to handle exceptions. Come up with the rules in advance regarding what would make you pause. Check if the assistant is picking the right or the wrong variant. If it's picking the wrong variants and you're not getting enough quality orders from the assistant, decide whether it's worth it in relation to your store's readiness and the effort that you will need to undertake to make it ready.

According to Adobe, visits to retail websites coming from AI are up by 127% in a year. That's as of August 2026. But visits are only one thing, and what an owner needs to know is a completely different thing: what those visitors buy and if they buy, and what's left after fees, discounts, returns, and most importantly, your team's time. If you are a relatively small store, use tagging to record where the order came from, and analyze this information at least once a month. The tags could be shipped, canceled, needed assistance, or came back as a repeat customer. If you are selling to several markets, analyze each market separately. The whole point is to understand if the amount of time you're going to invest in making your store ready is worth the number of new clients and increased sales you're going to make from all the improvements and changes.

Questions business owners ask

Do I need to rebuild my store for this?

Short answer, probably not. If you are considering paying for another integration, initially look at your catalog, the inventory, and what your store already shares. Check if your feed has any missing pieces, such as which van the part fits. Look at whether that information is already mentioned in the catalog. Usually only the link between the two needs fixing.

How do I keep the assistant from promising delivery we can't meet?

The assistant sees the exact item plus where the customer is. Your warehouse stock number is not enough. It has to say what exactly can ship to that particular address. Don't forget about availability and delivery terms. They need to be confirmed at the moment of the order.

Who handles the customer if they buy somewhere other than our site?

Get clarity on that before you switch it on. The buyer has to be provided all the information, such as who the seller is, your offer in detail, who can make edits to the order, and how the returns are processed. Figure out how competing offers are ranked. Make sure you can tell which orders came from an assistant, and check if those customers can be contacted later. If you offer cross-border selling, remember that terms differ from market to market, and that the EU AI Act has transparency rules for AI.

Going back to the initial question, can you find the heated mirror for the 2021 Sprinter, and can it be delivered by Friday?

If the answer only lives with someone who picks up the phone, this is where you should start.
